Preamble

I did this in 2024, and some friends found it useful so I'm back. This is to summarize my research and decisions for the June 2 ballot in San Francisco in the hopes of helping others decide who to vote for.

Federal

U.S. Representative, District 11

Saikat Chakrabarti
  • If this were a ranked ballot, mine would be 1. Wiener 2. Chakrabarti. But unfortunately we don't have ranked-choice voting for this office yet. So the upshot is if you lean  Scott Wiener and aren't interested in my strategic-vote logic, just go ahead and vote for him.
  • The three viable candidates are Scott Wiener, Connie Chan, and Saikat Chakrabarti.
  • Chan is out because she's inexperienced and a NIMBY. While I somewhat worry about Chakrabarti's experience (highest level has been AOC's chief of staff), I definitely worry about Chan's. She has been against a bunch of things I've supported in the city in recent years: she wanted to weaken the Family Zoning Plan and was against implementing bus rapid transit on Geary which has been great on the 49 Van Ness/Mission and would have been great on the 38 Geary as well.
  • My vote is essentially predicated on a gamble that it's going to come down to a race between Chakrabarti and Chan for second place to advance to the general election. This worry is borne out by the fact that Chakrabarti and Chan are neck-and-neck for second place in some polls: 20% vs 17% in a poll by Chakrabarti's campaign and 18% vs 17% in a poll from the Chron. I am essentially voting Chakrabarti to ensure that Chan is locked out of the general election, with the hope that the progressive vote will coalesce around Chakrabarti rather than Chan.
  • Wiener and Chakrabarti are practically identical in policy preference, with essentially two exceptions: Chakrabarti has placed a bigger emphasis on campaign-finance reform (notably advocating for a ban on Congressional stock trading at the start of his campaign) and distinguished himself as the more pro-Palestine candidate. (You can read Wiener's and Chakrabarti's positions there.)
  • The decision here thus depends on what you see the role of San Francisco's Congressional representative to be in the post-Pelosi era:
    • Chakrabarti excels in talking about poltiics on the national stage. Particularly as part of a wider progressive/DSA movement (AOC, Bernie, Zohran, etc.). I've met him and heard him speak. I even volunteered for his campaign before Wiener entered the race. I suggest signing up for a small group Zoom with him to ask him questions; I did this and it ended up being a 1:1 where he talked about his transition from tech to politics via /r/CodersForSanders a decade ago.
    • Wiener excels at "getting things done." He's clearly the more experienced legislator after spending years in the State Senate and San Francisco Board of Supervisors. And the things Wiener has done at the state level have been things I agree with as priorities:
      • Funding public transit: he's the reason Connect Bay Area is even able to collect signatures.
      • Building more housing, even pushing for things like upzoning near transit (SB 79) when it was more controversial in the Democratic party.
  • So the natural question is: would a Rep. Wiener be able to get anything done with the current state of the federal government? It's reasonable to think not, although housing is somewhat uniquely bipartisan (see for example H.R. 6644). So if that's the case, who do you want asking questions in Congressional hearings and speaking as San Francisco's representative on the national stage? There's certainly an argument that Chakrabarti would excel more in this role from his experience in federal politics.
  • I am still salty about SB 1524, a bad bill that Scott Wiener passed that allows restaurants to lie about their menu pricing. The issue itself is irrelevant on the scope of Congress, but the notion that he would go against the will of San Franciscans (80% in a Chronicle survey supported the law he overturned) to side with industry lobbyists if he can get away with it doesn't bode well.

State

Governor

Katie Porter (but possibly Tom Steyer)
  • The Republicans are out: Bianco is an election denier, and Hilton doesn't believe humans are causing climate change.
  • There are a lot of candidates, so to vote strategically, I'm limiting my consideration to the top few highest polling candidates to avoid effectively wasting my vote on someone without a chance. (Why can't we have ranked choice voting statewide already?) That effectively leaves Becerra, Steyer, and Porter.
  • There are some pros/cons for each of these candidates:
    • Porter is a great communicator. You may know her from her whiteboard- and poster-based explanations in Congress. More recently, I was impressed with her performance at the gubernatorial debate. Downsides are she's had some minor scandals: snapping at a staffer, abruptly walking out of an interview, calling the US Senate election that she lost "rigged."
    • Steyer is the only one talking about Prop 13 reform, which is a critical issue. Also, PG&E is spending money against him, which is a major plus ("enemy of my enemy" as they say). The biggest downside is his lack of experience in government, which makes me worry about his ability to execute on his goals. (Prop 13 reform, for example, failed to pass in 2020 and I haven't seen a good response from Steyer about why a new referendum would be any different.)
    • Becerra is by far the most experienced (former California Attorney General and Biden's Secretary of Health and Human Services). But he seems to mostly be running on maintaining the status quo and citing his record without bringing many new ideas to the table. He's also worringly made a flip-flop on support for single-payer healthcare. Either position there is defensible, but I'd expect a candidate (especially the former HHS Secretary) to have a principled position there backed by their experience.
  • On critical issues like housing, the candidates seem to differ little: all candidates recognize that we need to build more housing to make it more affordable. Nobody was echoing NIMBY talking points about greedy developers conspiring to keep apartments vacant or anything like that.
  • On balance, I'm going with Porter because she's a great communicator, has some good policies, and has sufficient government experience via her role in Congress. (If it were ranked, I would vote 1. Porter 2. Steyer 3. Becerra.)
  • However, this gubernatorial race has been full of twists and turns. The frontrunner a month ago was Eric Swalwell, and nobody had heard of Becerra. So I'm going to hold off on voting until closer to Election Day. If it looks like Porter is trending down, I may shift over to Steyer.

Secretary of State

Shirley N. Weber (incumbent)
  • Incumbent who seems to be doing well.
  • As a rule of thumb, if GrowSF and the League of Pissed Off Voters agree on something, it's probably a safe vote. (Those two rarely agree.)

Controller

Malia M. Cohen (incumbent)
  • She's an incumbent who's doing fine and has broad support.

Treasurer

Eleni Kounalakis
  • Has sufficient experience: she is currently Lieutenant Governor
  • She worked in housing development before entering government, and this position has a role heading the state agencies that manage funding for affordable housing, which is also a plus.

Attorney General

Rob Bonta (incumbent)
  • Another incumbent doing well.
  • On housing issues: he has fought NIMBYs in Woodside by enforcing state housing laws.

Insurance Commissioner

Patrick Wolff
  • This is somewhat of a critical moment with insurance in California. With insurance companies leaving the state as a result of elevated wildfire risk, the insurance commissioner will play a critical role in ensuring that Californians continue to have access to insurance.
  • Wolff has relevant experience (working as a financial analyst analyzing insurance markets) and has committed to not running for any other office. This position should not be a political stepping stone as it has been in the past.

Board of Equalization Member, District 2

John Pimentel
  • The Board of Equalization shouldn't exist; almost all of its responsibilities were effectively stripped away in 2017. This is basically a position that does nothing but collect a salary of $184,447 and act as a political stepping stone.
  • Pimentel signalled openness to abolishing the Board and has relevant experience consolidating state agencies as deputy secretary of transportation.
  • The incumbent (Sally Lieber) is fine, but she also made a point of mentioning that she upholds Prop 13 in her candidate statement for some reason. But again, she doesn't because her job has no responsibilities.

June 2026 Voter Guide

By Nicholas Currault

Preamble

I did this in 2024, and some friends found it useful so I'm back. This is to summarize my research and decisions for the June 2 ballot in San Francisco in the hopes of helping others decide who to vote for.

Federal

U.S. Representative, District 11

Saikat Chakrabarti
  • If this were a ranked ballot, mine would probably be 1. Wiener, 2. Chakrabarti. But unfortunately we don't have ranked-choice voting for this office yet. So the upshot is if you lean  Scott Wiener and aren't interested in my strategic-vote logic, just go ahead and vote for him.
  • The three viable candidates are Scott Wiener, Connie Chan, and Saikat Chakrabarti.
  • Chan is out because she's inexperienced and a NIMBY. While I somewhat worry about Chakrabarti's experience (highest level has been AOC's chief of staff), I definitely worry about Chan's. She has been against a bunch of things I've supported in the city in recent years: she wanted to weaken the Family Zoning Plan and was against implementing bus rapid transit on Geary which has been great on the 49 Van Ness/Mission and would have been great on the 38 Geary as well.
  • My vote is essentially predicated on a gamble that it's going to come down to a race between Chakrabarti and Chan for second place to advance to the general election. This worry is borne out by the fact that Chakrabarti and Chan are neck-and-neck for second place in some polls: 20% vs 17% in a poll by Chakrabarti's campaign and 18% vs 17% in a poll from the Chron. I am essentially voting Chakrabarti to ensure that Chan is locked out of the general election, with the hope that the progressive vote will coalesce around Chakrabarti rather than Chan.
  • Wiener and Chakrabarti are practically identical in policy preference, with essentially two exceptions: Chakrabarti has placed a bigger emphasis on campaign-finance reform (notably advocating for a ban on Congressional stock trading at the start of his campaign) and distinguished himself as the more pro-Palestine candidate. (You can read Wiener's and Chakrabarti's positions there.)
  • The decision here thus depends on what you see the role of San Francisco's Congressional representative to be in the post-Pelosi era:
    • Chakrabarti excels in talking about politics on the national stage. Particularly as part of a wider progressive/DSA movement (AOC, Bernie, Zohran, etc.). I've met him and heard him speak. I even volunteered for his campaign before Wiener entered the race. I suggest signing up for a small group Zoom with him to ask him questions; I did this and it ended up being a 1:1 where he talked about his transition from tech to politics via the /r/CodersForSanders subreddit a decade ago.
    • Wiener excels at "getting things done." He's clearly the more experienced legislator after spending years in the State Senate and San Francisco Board of Supervisors. And the things Wiener has done at the state level have been things I agree with as priorities:
      • Funding public transit: he's the reason Connect Bay Area is even able to collect signatures.
      • Building more housing, even pushing for things like upzoning near transit (SB 79) when it was more controversial in the Democratic party.
  • So the natural question is: would a Rep. Wiener be able to get anything done with the current state of the federal government? It's reasonable to think not, although housing is somewhat uniquely bipartisan (see for example H.R. 6644). So if that's the case, who do you want asking questions in Congressional hearings and speaking as San Francisco's representative on the national stage? There's certainly an argument that Chakrabarti would excel more in this role from his experience in federal politics.
  • I am still salty about SB 1524, a bad bill that Scott Wiener sponsored that allows restaurants to lie about their menu pricing. The issue itself is irrelevant on the scope of Congress, but the notion that he would go against the will of San Franciscans (80% in a Chronicle survey supported the law he overturned) to side with industry lobbyists if he can get away with it doesn't bode well.

State

Governor

Katie Porter (but possibly Tom Steyer)
  • If you like BuzzFeed-style quizzes, try this one out.
  • The Republicans are out: Bianco is an election denier, and Hilton doesn't believe humans are causing climate change.
  • There are a lot of candidates, so to vote strategically, I'm limiting my consideration to the top few highest polling candidates to avoid effectively wasting my vote on someone without a chance. (Why can't we have ranked choice voting statewide already?) That effectively leaves Becerra, Steyer, and Porter.
  • There are some pros/cons for each of these candidates:
    • Porter is a great communicator. You may know her from her whiteboard- and poster-based explanations in Congress. More recently, I was impressed with her performance at the gubernatorial debate. Downsides are she's had some minor scandals: snapping at a staffer, abruptly walking out of an interview, calling the US Senate election that she lost "rigged."
    • Steyer is the only one talking about Prop 13 reform, which is a critical issue. Also, PG&E is spending money against him, which is a major plus. ("The enemy of my enemy is my friend," as they say.) The biggest downside is his lack of experience in government, which makes me worry about his ability to execute. (Prop 13 reform, for example, failed to pass in 2020 and I haven't seen a good response from Steyer about why a new referendum would be any different.)
    • Becerra is by far the most experienced (former California Attorney General and Biden's Secretary of Health and Human Services). But he seems to mostly be running on maintaining the status quo and citing his record without bringing many new ideas to the table. He's also worryingly flip-flopped on support for single-payer healthcare. Either position there is defensible, but I'd expect a candidate (especially the former HHS Secretary) to have a principled position there backed by their experience. Becerra is also pulling some campaign-finance shenanigans like red-boxing.
  • On critical issues like housing, the candidates seem to differ little: all candidates recognize that we need to build more housing to make it more affordable. Nobody was echoing NIMBY talking points about greedy developers conspiring to keep apartments vacant or anything like that. See Ezra Klein's housing forum (or this SF Standard article summarizing takeaways) for more details here.
  • On balance, I'm going with Porter because she's a great communicator, has some good policies, and has sufficient government experience via her role in Congress. (If it were ranked, I would vote 1. Porter, 2. Steyer, 3. Becerra.)
  • However, this gubernatorial race has been full of twists and turns. The frontrunner a month ago was Eric Swalwell, and nobody had heard of Becerra. So I'm going to hold off on voting until closer to Election Day. If it looks like Porter is trending down and has no chance, I may shift over to Steyer.

Lieutenant Governor

Michael Tubbs
  • He has government experience as former mayor of Stockton, where he piloted a few successful programs:
    • A direct cash assistance program that was successful.
    • A controversial but ultimately successful program aimed at preventing gun violence by connecting likely perpetrators with a stipend and resources to "build a life outside of gangs, guns, and the underground economy."
  • He testified in support of transit-oriented development and CEQA reform, even when these issues were more politically controversial. From this testimony, he also seems like a good communicator.
  • Lieutenant Governor doesn't do a whole lot, but they are an ex officio member of some important boards like the UC Regents.

Secretary of State

Shirley N. Weber (incumbent)
  • Incumbent who seems to be doing well.
  • As a rule of thumb, if GrowSF and the League of Pissed Off Voters agree on something, it's probably a safe vote. (Those two rarely agree.)

Controller

Malia M. Cohen (incumbent)
  • She's an incumbent who's doing fine and has broad support.

Treasurer

Eleni Kounalakis
  • Has sufficient experience: she is currently Lieutenant Governor
  • She worked in housing development before entering government, and this position has a role heading the state agencies that manage funding for affordable housing, which is also a plus.

Attorney General

Rob Bonta (incumbent)
  • Another incumbent doing well.
  • On housing issues: he has fought NIMBYs in Woodside by enforcing state housing laws.

Insurance Commissioner

Patrick Wolff
  • This is somewhat of a critical moment with insurance in California. With insurance companies leaving the state as a result of elevated wildfire risk, the insurance commissioner will play a critical role in ensuring that Californians continue to have access to insurance.
  • Wolff has relevant experience (working as a financial analyst analyzing insurance markets) and has committed to not running for any other office. This position should not be a political stepping stone as it has been in the past.

Board of Equalization Member, District 2

John Pimentel
  • The Board of Equalization shouldn't exist; almost all of its responsibilities were effectively stripped away in 2017. This is basically a position that does nothing but collect a salary of $184,447 and act as a political stepping stone.
  • Pimentel signaled openness to abolishing the Board and has relevant experience consolidating state agencies as deputy secretary of transportation.
  • The incumbent (Sally Lieber) is fine, but she also made a point of mentioning that she upholds Prop 13 in her candidate statement for some reason. But again, her job has no responsibilities, so it doesn't really matter.

State Assembly Member, District 17

Matt Haney (incumbent)
  • He's the only one on the ballot...
  • Broadly doing well in the assembly, like this bill to facilitate office-to-residential conversions (which became law)

Superintendent of Public Instruction

Josh Newman
  • The two viable candidates seem to be Newman and Barrera.
  • Newman served as Chair of the State Senate Committee on Education; Barrera is the current superintendent's advisor (and was previously on the San Diego School Board).
  • Broadly, Newman seems to be running on how to make schools better while Barrera's statement seems to imply that we're in great shape and merely need to keep up the good work. ("In California, we’re First in Reading, Math, Science, Graduation Rates and College/Career readiness. We’ve achieved success by...") But Newman emphasizes the need for improvement.
  • Newman also thinks this position should be appointed, which I am inclined to agree with given that I do not feel like I know enough about education to help make this decision.

Local

Board of Education (“School Board”) Member

Phil Kim (incumbent)
  • SFUSD teachers recently went on strike for the first time in 50 years.
  • Kim is an (appointed) incumbent who seems to be doing well: Phil Kim was unanimously re-elected board president.
  • Kim has experience as a STEM educator and supports teaching algebra in 8th grade (which is somehow controversial?)
  • From the Chronicle's interviews, other candidates Cheung and Marckmann seem to lack the knowledge necessary to deal with budget shortfalls and school closures (and resulting resource reallocation) that SFUSD is facing.

Judge Of The Superior Court, Seat #16

Phoebe Maffei
  • Both candidates (Maffei and Pray) are good choices who were rated as “well qualified” by the bar association.
  • Maffei has experience as a prosecutor (including successfully prosecuting the man who attacked Paul Pelosi in 2022) while Pray has a similar amount of experience (~15 years) as a public defender.
  • Both candidates emphasized to the Chronicle that the race shouldn't be thought of through the typical lens of a prosecutor's versus a public defender's political agendas.
  • I ended up on Maffei because she seems to have a slightly broader experience (including with things like white-collar crime) and indicated more flexibility in judicial seat assignment. But I agree that they are both good choices.

Local Measures

Measure A: Earthquake Safety and Emergency Response Bond

YES
  • Standard infrastructure bond with broad support

Measure B: Lifetime Term Limits for Mayor and Members of the Board of Supervisors

NO
  • Despite being massively popular, pretty much all available political-science literature indicates term limits for legislators (like the Board of Supervisors) is a bad idea.
    • An analysis of state-level term limits found that term-limited legislators in their last term "sponsor fewer bills, are less productive on committees, and are absent for more floor votes."
    • Term limits increase the power of unelected bureaucrats (like non-term-limited staffers who bounce between legislators) and lobbyists for special interests.
    • Other studies have also shown that term limits increase political polarization.
  • I would actually support rolling back San Francisco's term limits that were initially passed in 1990
    • This seems to be a pattern of others that try term limits. Other states have tried term limits and then extended the limits or repealed them entirely.
    • On a city level, Los Angeles extended its city-council term limits 20 years ago. We should at the very least mirror them (3 terms instead of 2).
  • This is clearly moving in the wrong direction on term limits. It's also a little wacky in that it's not solving an actual problem. And even if it is, it's a problem that has occurred exactly once: only one Supervisor (Aaron Peskin) has even served non-consecutive terms.

Measure C: Decreases to Business Taxes

NO
  • This measure essentially tweaks business taxes in a broadly sensible way: it basically indexes the small-business exemption from 2024 Prop M.
  • The changes could be fine, but I agree with SPUR's analysis that Prop M hasn't been in force long enough for there to be any evidence on how it's working.
  • This is a revenue-negative change at a time when we're already losing city services (especially in healthcare) due to recent federal cuts from H.R. 1.
  • The same people who supported Prop M are supporting this: Rodney Fong was a proponent on M in 2024 and is a proponent on C now. And their campaign funders also overlap significantly (see 2024 Prop M and this election's Prop C on sfethics.org.)
  • Why are they annoying us voters with another business tax change when we just passed their last one? If they wanted this change they should have consolidated to save us on election costs.
  • As far as I can tell, the petition to put this on the ballot was circulated using paid petition gatherers without a grassroots signature gathering campaign, which is a negative in my book. Paid gatherers' tactics are problematic, and while arguably a necessary evil given ballot thresholds, I feel that someone who doesn't run a campaign without at least a grassroots component is going against the intent of the initiative system. They spent $399K on petition circulation (PCI Campaigns, Inc).

Measure D: Increases to Business Tax Based on Comparison of Top Executive's Pay to Employee's Pay

NO
  • This tax measure is a very drastic increase that would raise the Overpaid Executive Gross Receipts Tax by 9 times. It's worth being cautious about such an increase.
  • Although proponents pitch it as a way to incentivize more equitable pay structures, it's hard to imagine a city-level tax having an impact on the calculus of something like a national grocery chain.
  • Oddly enough, the companies more affected by the Overpaid Executive Tax aren't the tech companies with all the money. Think about the median tech company employee: it's probably a software engineer making a relatively high salary. But Safeway? Probably a cashier making much less. So the companies hit with the highest tax rate aren't the companies that the campaign seems to highlight. GrowSF did an analysis, and while it's not necessarily correct (as we don't know the method that will be used to evaluate executive pay), it shows how the impact could fall more on businesses that aren't really highlighted in the campaign.
  • Again, as far as I can tell, the petition to put this on the ballot was circulated using paid petition gatherers without a grassroots signature gathering campaign. Specifically, they spent $240K on petition circulation (Pen to Paper Canvassing, Inc).

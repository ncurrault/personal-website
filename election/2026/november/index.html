Supervisor, District 8

  1. Gary McCoy
  2. Darshini Patel
  3. Michael Nguyen
  • Gary McCoy is without a doubt the best candidate in my book.
    • Experience: he has been an aide for Nancy Pelosi and Scott Wiener. He has also led both of SF's LGBTQ Democratic clubs (Alice B. Toklas and Harvey Milk). And furthermore, he got here after battling addiction and homelessness years ago, so he is uniquely qualified to provide a perspective on making these systems work for similarly-situated San Franciscans.
    • Housing: he's firmly a YIMBY who believes in building "housing at every level." Furthermore, I attended a candidate forum in which he even tossed out the idea of using eminent domain to seize and build dense housing on the site of the Castro Safeway. Which is a bit extreme but I love where his heart is: he can probably start by figuring out how to consolidate the Safeway and its parking lot down from 10 parcels straddling Reservoir Street down to something more manageable, but I'm confident this is an idea a Supervisor McCoy would be receptive to.
    • Civil liberties: McCoy has pitched a plan to end the city's contract with Flock Safety as it comes up for renewal in February 2027. Furthermore, he also wants to tighten rules about use of license-plate data to prevent similar concerns from cropping up with other surveillance technologies. We need to do everything we can to get Flock out of the city as it continues to cause civil-liberties concerns.
    • Castro-specific issues: McCoy supports extending last call to 4am and extending transit to facilitate late-night transportation to the area.
    • I feel like McCoy is, in a way, the perfect candidate for this moment. He's able to cut through the usual quasi-partisan conflict in SF to earn endorsements spanning from SF YIMBY to the League of Pissed Off Voters.
  • Darshini Patel is also a good choice.
    • Patel seems to have been late to the campaign game — I only heard about her candidacy a couple of weeks ago — but her policies seem to mostly echo what I like about McCoy's (she's similarly pro-housing, for example), and the two candidates have co-endorsed each other.
    • Patel has certainly done her homework, but I'm still leaning towards McCoy because his experience is more local while Patel's is more national (working in the White House under Obama and on Harris's presidential campaign).
  • I disagree with Michael Nguyen on some local policy, but he seems like a good person, so I would still be comfortable with him in the role.
    • I have heard from a close friend who ran into and had a casual conversation with him that he was super nice and receptive to ideas.
    • Nguyen's housing platform seems to be confined to only social housing. Social housing is a good idea, but inclusionary zoning that he supports (and calls out other candidates for opposing) is not really the same thing and is essentially a tax on new housing with limited benefits. Overall, I worry that Nguyen will follow a pattern I have seen with other local politicans who support some but not all types of housing, which I do not think will suffice to solve our present housing crisis.
  • In case you haven't heard the news, Emanuel "Manny" Yekutiel has been accused of sexual assault not just once but twice and has subsequently suspended his campaign after many people and groups dropped their endorsements and called on him to do so. So um, he's out.

Board of Education (“School Board”) Member

Phil Kim   Tim Tung   Alida Fisher  
  • Note: You can vote for up to three candidates here.
  • I am supporting Kim for the same reasons as in June.
  • Tung is an SFUSD parent with a background managing public finances for the City of San Jose. Given SFUSD's seemingly perennial budget issues, this background seems relevant to the school board. Plus he obviously has a vested interest in SFUSD's success given his daughters attend SFUSD schools.
  • Looking a mutual endorsements on SF Endorsements, Autumn Brown Garibay seems to be third candidate in this quasi-slate. While Garibay is active in the PTA and has three kids in SFUSD schools, I cannot find any information suggesting they have relevant policy experience for school board. Because of this, I am sticking with Alida Fisher, an incumbent who seems to be relatively uncontroversial.

Community College Board

Elijah Ball   Erwin Tam   Leah LaCroix  
  • Note: You can vote for up to three candidates here.
  • My primary motivation here was selecting candidates who have experience working in community-college policy or direct experience at CCSF to ensure that its Board of Trustees will have relevant experience.
  • Elijah Ball and Erwin Tam have experience advising community colleges on budget issues.
  • Most Ball/Tam endorsers have also endorsed Monroe Lace; they are effectively running together as a slate. However, while she does have experience as a teacher, I am nervous that she seems to lack experience working directly with CCSF or other community colleges.
  • For my third vote, I wavered between Rome Moses Jones and Leah LaCroix. Rome Moses Jones seems like a good choice (CCSF alumnus with nonprofit experience) but does not really have a platform and it seems that his campaign never really got off the ground. That said, I like that he is running a small campaign that exists outside of the usual city-politics machinery. Leah LaCroix from the progressive slate took dual-enrollment courses at CCSF and has policy experience. I ended up deciding on LaCroix because she has a history of advocating for transit and wants to priotitize "free transit passes for students" (which I assume is in reference to the Bay Pass pilot program).

Proposition 37: Creates Loan Program for Middle-Income Buyers of Qualified New Homes

YES
  • Creates a program to help certain qualified Californians receive downpayment assistance when buying a home.
  • At first, this looks like a demand subsidy for housing which would do little to address housing affordability, but looking closer it's actually an incentive mechanism for building new housing: the government loans can only be used on new homes. So developers may be incentivized to build more as a result because it essentially expands the pool of who might be able to buy the homes they're building.
  • I'm probably voting yes on anything with even a little potential to create more housing, and this fits that bill.

Proposition 40: Billionaire Tax

NO
  • A wealth tax like this one (even a one-time one levied at the state level) is indeed tempting. Our tax system is unfair, and every attempt to "make the rich pay their fair share" tends to place more of a burden on high-earning W2 professionals via income tax. (TODO elaborate, also mention SDI cap removal)
  • A yes vote here is also tempting because this is indeed trying to address an emergency TODO summarize healthcare cuts and SEIU's response. This SEIU context is also worth pausing to consider: it's worth noting that this is coming from one specific union with its own set of interests. This isn't necessarily disqualifying, but it's worth noting because it made other unions unhappy TODO. One counter-argument to the proposal is that the union is attempting to tap into a TODO economic anger(?) in order to secure revenue.
  • I am voting no because the authors of this proposal essentially managed to make every possible mistake in drafting it and end up with a wealth tax that's deeply flawed. The proposal is so poorly drafted that and even if it does somehow manage to survive court and implementation challenges and go into effect, I do not find it very likely that it will even accomplish its goals.
    • It's an ex post facto law
    • It doesn't actually tax wealth
    • The "optional deferral account" scheme means much of the revenue will be long delayed TODO
  • As an aside, I want to add that a lot of the reasons other opponents are bringing up about this measure are not very compelling.
    • Detractors also argue that the "one-time" framing is misleading because it will inevitably result in more wealth taxes being levied, potentially with higher rates and lower wealth thresholds. This is the slippery-slope fallacy. If this were to pass and go into effect, and Californians wanted to try to institute a second "one-time" wealth tax or a more continuous wealth-tax scheme, they would have to return to the ballot box. And at that time, it would be a fundamentally different debate because rather than considering a hypothetical as we are now, we would be able to look back at the empirical effects of this measure to inform what the new measure would effect.
    • The official argument makes the tired argument that it will cause capital to leave the state. This gets thrown around virtually any time a tax increase is proposed but has rarely actually happened in recent years. (TODO cite MA example) And in the case of this proposition, we don't even need to speculate because due to the aforementioned retroactivity provision, anyone who would leave and establish residency elsewhere has already done so. Capital flight is a risk worth considering, but at the end of the day California's economy has so much going for it that TODO. Perhaps trying out a novel tax will cause uncertainty about future tax policy which can in turn have negative economic consequences, however. This would be the first attempt of a wealth tax that I am aware of in the United States, so of course nobody actually knows the full scope of economic effects it will cause.
  • Some better ideas to direct energy towards for how to tax billionaires that people considering a yes vote should advocate for:
    • Repeal or at least reform 1978 prop 13. The Los Angeles Country Club does not to continue to receive a massive taxpayer subsidy. Same deal with billionaire homeowners or those who own multiple homes.
    • Tax equity-collateralized loans. This is essentially a loophole that allows billionaires to realize their gains without ever technically selling equity and incurring capital-gains taxes buy borrowing against their holdings and only paying interest.

Proposition 41: Poison pill for billionaire tax

NO
  • This is basically a confusing audit requirement that would have little actual effect except to prevent prop 40 from going into effect if both passed and this one earns more YES votes. That's not really how democracy should work. Even though I disagree with prop 40, as a matter of principle "poison pill" ballot measures like this are an awful way to set policy.

Proposition 42: Another poison pill for the billionaire tax

NO
  • Did opponents of prop 40 really need two separate poison pills? This one highlights that the tax woudld be retroactive, which is one of the reasons I oppose it (see my prop 40 argument), but once again poison pills are a bad idea. A simple ballot argument mentioning retroactivity being a bad idea would have been fine, but for some reason prop 40 detractors want to waste everyone's time and taxpayer money on making ballots and election guides longer.
  • Whether prop 40 passes should be based on how voters vote on prop 40, not a bunch of unrelated confusingly worded measures.

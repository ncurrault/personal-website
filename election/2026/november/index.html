State Propositions

Proposition 1: Bonds for Housing Affordability Programs

YES
  • Note: Public bonds like this one need to be referred to the ballot by the legislature.
  • Money for building housing, yay!
  • Relatively uncontroversial bond measure. I cannot find anyone in SF endorsing NO, and there's not even an official opponent argument in the state voter guide.

Proposition 2: Increase to State “Rainy Day” Fund

YES
  • Note: This is a state constitutional amendment referred to the ballot by the legislature.
  • In a nutshell, due to California's progressive tax code, tax revenue can be inconsistent due to dependency on stock market performance. The rainy day fund smooths that out a bit by allowing the state to save money in good years to then spend in bad ones.
  • Relatively little opposition, having passed the state legislature by a wide margin and being endorsed by practically everyone.

Proposition 3: Extend Tax on Incomes Above $371,000

YES
  • Endorsed by practically everyone
  • This tax passed in 2012, but it is due to expire in a few years. This would effectively remove that sunset provision.
  • The only argument against is that CA ballot propositions in general can be a “slippery slope” where a policy is passed with a sunset provision to make it seem less risky and then the sunset provision is later removed. (This was an argument against Prop 50 last year.) But this doesn't really pass muster: trying out a policy, evaluating it, and then making it permanent is a perfectly reasonable governing practice.

Proposition 4: Allow Public Financing for Campaigns

YES
  • This would repeal a prohibition in the state constitution against public campaign financing. Due to the way the ban affects charter cities, the ban does not actually apply to charter cities like San Francisco, so SF already has a public financing program.
  • Public campaign financing is a topic too broad to cover here, but essentially proponents argue it helps boost smaller campaigns by matching small donations with taxpayer funds to effectively tip the scales in favor of smaller campaigns as if they had raised more money than they did. Opponents argue that taxpayer funds should not be used for political campaigns or that public financing does not produce meaningful results.
  • Whether we should have public campaign financing and the specifics of such systems is a policy debate that we should have on the state and local level without a blanket prohibition. Even though there are some arguments against, the arguments are not persuasive enough to necessitate a constitutional ban on the practice. Detractors should simply advocate for not creating public financing systems when writing relevant statutes.

Proposition 5: Changes to Recall Process

YES
  • Endorsed by practically everyone
  • Currently (as you may be aware if you were here in 2021 for the Newsom recall election), state recall elections are two-part:
    1. Should X official be recalled?
    2. If they are recalled, select their replacement from these candidates.
  • This is a terrible system. For starters, the simultaneous election is done without ranked-choice voting or even a primary process. So taking the 2021 recall as an example, if the four major Republicans running had split the vote evenly, one could have become governor with only 25% of the vote. Furthermore, because of the two-step vote (as GrowSF notes), if a recalled official loses by a single vote, it does not matter that the votes for their replacement could be dwarfed by the number of NO votes on the recall. Effectively, the current election process can replace the governor with someone else who is massively less popular than the incumbent as determined by preferences within the recall election itself.
  • The new system is much more reasonable and mirrors what we already do for city elections. Essentially, recall is one yes/no question. If they are recalled, we then follow existing processes for death and disability of officers: the governor appoints a temporary replacement until a special election can be held. For the office of governor, the Lieutenant Governor would then temporarily serve as governor until the special election or (if it happened late in the term) until the following general election.
  • The one opponent I could find here is the Green Party who does helpfully point out that we should be using ranked-choice voting for elections. Which I 100% agree with! But perfect should not be the enemy of the good here.

Proposition 37: Creates Loan Program for Middle-Income Buyers of Qualified New Homes

YES
  • No opponent argument was submitted.
  • Creates a program to help certain qualified Californians receive down payment assistance when buying a home.
  • At first, this looks like a demand subsidy for housing which would do little to address housing affordability, but looking closer it's actually an incentive mechanism for building new housing: the government loans can only be used on new homes. So developers may be incentivized to build more as a result because it essentially expands the pool of who might be able to buy the homes they're building.
  • I'm probably voting yes on anything with even a little potential to create more housing, and this fits that bill.

Proposition 38: Bonds for Immunology Medical Research

NO
  • This is a bit of a weird one: of course we should fund medical research, but why was this run as an initiative campaign and not simply through the legislature?
  • These are not just general bonds to fund research: they would effectively mostly go to the institute chaired by the billionaire funding this measure.
  • Funding medical research is obviously a priority, but we have processes for deciding what the public should fund for a reason, and these processes should not involve asking every Californian to weigh in by running an election. In a nutshell, this is bad policy, and if this passes it will only encourage other research institutes to try their own initiative petitions to get funding.

Proposition 39: Voter ID

NO
  • This is an initiative to require voter ID in California. Voter ID is almost always a bad idea intended to disenfranchise specific voters. It's a solution in search of a problem, given that (despite what certain politicians like to say), the type of noncitizen voting targeted by voter ID measures is extremely rare.
  • Giving proponents the benefit of the doubt, the impulse to use IDs to ensure elections remain secure is a valid one. But we need more overhauling before we can get there: if IDs aren't issued for free to everyone, they would effectively be an indirect poll tax. If proponents could first verify that everyone can obtain an acceptable form of ID easily and at no cost and with no disparate impact, then we can discuss adding a requirement to vote.
  • Another objection is they want people to write their SSN on the outside of vote-by-mail envelopes where anyone can see it (“Invalidates mail ballots that do not have last four digits of designated government-issued identification number written on envelope”). Which … yikes?

Proposition 40: SEIU-UHW West's Billionaire Tax

NO
  • A wealth tax like this one (even a one-time one levied at the state level) is indeed tempting. Our tax system is unfair, and every attempt to use income taxes to “make the rich pay their fair share” tends to place more of a burden on high-earning W2 professionals via income tax. (See, for example, the recent removal of the income cap for the SDI payroll tax.)

  • A yes vote here is also tempting because this is indeed trying to address an emergency. From the proponent statement in the state voter guide:

    Trump’s “Big, Ugly Bill” slashed funding for healthcare in California to pay for more billionaire tax breaks, eliminating coverage for over a million Californians and doubling health insurance premiums for millions more. Vote YES on Prop. 40 to make billionaires finally pay their fair share in taxes and stop sticking the middle class with the bill.

    HR 1 — I will absolutely never call it the “One Big Beautiful Bill” because that's perhaps the dumbest name for a law in US history — did indeed do what SEIU-UHW is describing, so that gives Prop 40 a certain symmetry: tax and spend in a way that directly cancels out HR 1.

    However, this SEIU-UHW context is also worth pausing to consider: it's worth noting that this is coming from one specific union with its own set of interests. This isn't necessarily disqualifying, but it's worth noting because it made other unions unhappy: teachers and firefighters oppose this measure. One counter-argument to the proposal is that SEIU-UHW is attempting to tap into a broader populist anger in order to secure revenue for itself.

  • I am voting no because the authors of this proposal essentially managed to make every possible mistake in drafting it and end up with a wealth tax that's deeply flawed. The proposal is so poorly drafted that even if it does somehow manage to survive court and implementation challenges and go into effect, I do not find it very likely that it will even accomplish its goals.
    • It's basically an ex post facto law
    • It doesn't actually tax wealth. It taxes equity proportional to voting rights, not revenue rights. This seems like more a specific middle finger to how tech founders organize their equity without any policy basis.
    • The “optional deferral account” (ODA) scheme means much of the revenue will be long delayed. In many cases (such as equity in private companies), taxpayers can essentially track a tax debt separately until assets in the ODA are liquid. Therefore, much of the revenue will not even be realized right away as proponents claim.
  • As an aside, I want to add that a lot of the reasons other opponents are bringing up about this measure are not very compelling.
    • Detractors also argue that the “one-time” framing is misleading because it will inevitably result in more wealth taxes being levied, potentially with higher rates and lower wealth thresholds. This is the slippery-slope fallacy. If this were to pass and go into effect, and Californians wanted to try to institute a second “one-time” wealth tax or a more continuous wealth-tax scheme, they would have to return to the ballot box. And at that time, it would be a fundamentally different debate because rather than considering a hypothetical as we are now, we would be able to look back at the empirical effects of this measure to inform what the new measure would effect.

    • The official argument makes the tired argument that it will cause capital to leave the state. This gets thrown around virtually any time a tax increase is proposed, but in the case of this proposition, we don't even need to speculate because due to the aforementioned retroactivity provision, anyone who would leave and establish residency elsewhere has already done so.

      Capital flight is a risk worth considering, but at the end of the day California's economy has so much going for it that I don't really buy this. Silicon Valley is here. A huge number of top-tier universities and the best cities in the country that people want to live in are here. California has so much going for it that the emphasis on fears of capital flight seems a tad alarmist.

      Perhaps trying out a novel tax will cause uncertainty about future tax policy which can in turn have negative economic consequences, however. This would be the first attempt at a wealth tax that I am aware of in the United States, so of course nobody actually knows the full scope of economic effects it will cause.

  • Some better ideas to direct energy towards for how to tax billionaires that people considering a yes vote should advocate for:
    • Repeal or at least reform 1978 Prop 13. The Los Angeles Country Club does not need to continue to receive a massive taxpayer subsidy. Same deal with billionaire homeowners or those who own multiple homes.
    • Tax equity-collateralized loans. This is essentially a loophole that allows billionaires to realize their gains without ever technically selling equity and incurring capital-gains taxes by borrowing against their holdings and only paying interest.
    • Literally just pass a more sound version of this same type of policy. I don't oppose a wealth tax in general, even at the state level. I view this NO vote as more of an indictment of the initiative system: it is no surprise that policies drafted behind closed doors like this are flawed. My hope is that this policy fails and that all of the discourse surrounding it is used to inform the authors of an analogous but more sound policy in the future. The enthusiasm behind this policy is real, and hopefully it can be tapped into in the future.

Proposition 41: Poison Pill for the Billionaire Tax

NO
  • This is basically a confusing audit requirement that would have little actual effect except to prevent Prop 40 from going into effect if both passed and this one earns more YES votes. That's not really how democracy should work. Even though I disagree with Prop 40, as a matter of principle “poison pill” ballot measures like this are an awful way to set policy.

Proposition 42: Another Poison Pill for the Billionaire Tax

NO
  • Did opponents of Prop 40 really need two separate poison pills? This one highlights that the tax would be retroactive, which is one of the reasons I oppose it (see my Prop 40 argument), but once again poison pills are a bad idea. A simple ballot argument mentioning retroactivity being a bad idea would have been fine, but for some reason Prop 40 detractors want to waste everyone's time and taxpayer money on making ballots and election guides longer.
  • Whether Prop 40 passes should be based on how voters vote on Prop 40, not a bunch of unrelated confusingly worded measures.

Proposition 43: Limits Voters’ Ability To Raise Revenues For Local Government Services

NO
  • Let's explain the loophole that this measure would close. Because one clause of the state constitution says that tax increases need to earn 2/3 of votes to pass but another says that initiative petitions that qualify for the ballot via signature only need 50%, the California Supreme Court had to resolve the ambiguity and decided that initiative taxes only need 50%. See California Cannabis Coalition v. City of Upland (Cal. 2017).
  • This is a weird loophole, but this closes it in the wrong direction. Really we should, in descending order of priority:
    1. Repeal or reform Prop 13 so we can just use property taxes instead of a patchwork of local taxes to fund local government services.
    2. Remove the 2/3 requirement for local taxes entirely
    3. Keep the current loophole system I guess. It worked for getting props H and RTM on the ballot, which I strongly support (see below).
  • The core of my policy position is that taxes aren't something that should need a supermajority. We don't even need a supermajority to amend the state constitution or city charter, but we do for changing one aspect of government policy? The goal here is obviously to lower taxes, but this is a bad policy approach. If you oppose new taxes, you should get voters to vote no when they are proposed.

Proposition 44: Requires Health Clinics to Spend 90% on Program Services

NO
  • A solution in search of a problem. Local health clinics wasting money is not really an issue, and there are real concerns that the added fines and administrative overhead could hurt these clinics.
  • Like the dialysis measures from recent years, this is another instance in SEIU-UHW West's “history of cynically leveraging marginalized Californians as bargaining chips”. It's possible that SEIU-UHW West wants some of these clinics to join its union, they refused, and this is just SEIU's retaliation.
  • As my usual rule goes, if GrowSF and the League of Pissed Off Voters are both against something, it's probably pretty bad.

Proposition 45: CEQA Review Modifications

NO
  • The California Environmental Quality Act (CEQA) is a 1970 law aimed at protecting the environment. However, it was passed in an era where protecting the environment meant stopping things from happening (cutting down trees, building big freeways through downtowns, etc.). This bill has come under fire more in recent years because now we are in the era of climate change. What's best for the environment is building things: Wind and solar farms; dense, transit-oriented development; and more infill housing to prevent suburban sprawl. But opponents have used CEQA lawsuits to effectively block much of these things. Some of these lawsuits definitely stretch the logic of the bill and the definition of the word “environment.” A famous case out of Berkeley ruled that noise from Cal students was part of the “environment” and thus needed to be assessed as part of CEQA environmental reviews in order to build housing in Berkeley.
  • One thing to keep in mind while evaluating CEQA arguments is that a project delayed is a project denied. Many major projects rely on time-limited financing, so a court challenge to a CEQA review that drags on can effectively be used as a heckler's veto to block a project.
  • My conflict here is that there are many projects that should not be subject to CEQA-based blocks, but on the other hand there some that still should. It's a cost-benefit analysis. CEQA must be tailored to avoid blocking projects critical to affordability like housing, public transit, and high-speed rail. But that does not mean we should throw the baby out with the bathwater and prevent CEQA from being used to challenge (for example) unpopular data centers that may be deleterious to the environment while providing little in the way of benefit. (As a disclaimer, whether data centers would be covered by the language of the proposition is still an open question.)
  • The ball is already rolling on CEQA amendments necessary to facilitate housing construction. AB 130 passed just last year and already added a bunch of CEQA exemptions for housing projects. There is no reason to think that YIMBYs in the legislature aren't capable of reforming CEQA the old-fashioned way: why do we need to go to the ballot box?
  • The proposition would make legislative amendments to CEQA require a 2/3 vote, so it could actually backfire and end up making it harder to further streamline CEQA insofar as housing and transit projects are concerned.
  • SPUR and California YIMBY are conflicted for similar reasons. This is a bit shocking because proponents of the proposition call out housing specifically, but these two extremely pro-housing groups could not reach consensus. From the YIMBY analysis:
    Whether this is a good idea depends on how you weigh the tradeoffs: is permitting new wind and solar energy infrastructure faster worth making it easier to build freeways? … These are big fundamental questions about growth, sustainability, and the proper structure of democratic decision-making.
  • Some useful interviews with major proponents and opponents of the measure on Political Breakdown by KQED that I used in evaluating my vote:
    • “Housing Fix or Data Center Loophole? This Ballot Measure Would Overhaul CEQA” (September 22, 2026)
    • “Will Prop. 45 Fix California’s Housing Crisis or Roll Back a Valuable Environmental Law?” (September 29, 2026)
  • In the end, I'm conflicted enough that my “when in doubt, vote no” philosophy on ballot propositions came into play. Especially given the high bar to legislatively amend CEQA that this proposition would create, I do not think this is a good use of the initiative system.

City and County Propositions

Proposition A: Charter Changes Affecting Various City Departments and Commissions

YES
  • Back in 2024, San Francisco created a commission to evaluate how to reduce its number of commissions. Although I thought it was a bit ironic at the time, this seems to have basically worked: said commission came back with a plan to eliminate, consolidate, and recharacterize commissions.
  • SPUR's guide has a good reasoning.

Proposition B: Establishing a Municipal Finance Corporation and a Public Bank

YES
  • There has been a push for public banks in California in recent years. This would basically be a bank owned by the public instead of the usual private ownership structure. Something like this already exists: the Bank of North Dakota.
  • It's an experiment worth trying: it can facilitate construction of affordable housing. And it's a time-limited offer: 2019 AB 857 is a state law that provides a regulatory structure for public banks in California, and it only gives local jurisdictions like SF seven years from the promulgation of relevant regulations to establish a bank and receive a license (Government Code § 57607(d)).
  • This prop wouldn't actually establish or fund the bank, but it would lay down the structure for one.
  • Potentially compelling opponent arguments:
    • SPUR notes that embedding governance structures in the City Charter is bad governance. Which may be true, but because this is a time limited offer, I think rejecting B would be misinterpreted as a lack of public support for the bank.
    • GrowSF notes that AB 857 requires public banks to obtain FDIC insurance and proceeds to claim without evidence that this is impossible. They're citing a random blog post about American Samoa and the fact that the Bank of North Dakota lacks FDIC insurance, not any actual FDIC policy against insuring public banks. These logical reasoning flaws push me to advocate for disregarding GrowSF's endorsement entirely.

    – Black Bird Bookstore & Cafe’s sales are up 44%, with book and coffee sales up 60% on Tuesdays, historically its slowest day of the week.
  • Minor pet peeve: proponents open with language (“Since the Great Highway closed...”) but the Great Highway is not closed. It's closed to private cars. They don't even for a moment consider that the Great Highway is now more accessible to SF residents like me who don't own a car. This indicates that the proponents are carbrains who do not even consider the experiences of residents who do not own cars.
